<div class="research-grid-container">
    <div class="research-grid-item research-grid-span-2">The evolution of NLR (nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat) genes in <em>Arabidopsis thaliana</em> and its relatives reflects adaptation to microbial threats, with these resistance (R) genes key to detecting pathogen effectors and triggering immunity. We investigate the ecological, genomic, and functional drivers of R gene evolution by studying NLR-effector interactions across global and local populations.</div>
    <div class="research-grid-item">
        <img src="../assets/images/NLR-Image2.jpg" alt="Placeholder Image" />
    </div>
    <div class="research-grid-item">
    <p>
To uncover the forces shaping plant immune system evolution, we investigate natural variation in NLR genes across three co-occurring Brassicaceae species—<em>Arabidopsis thaliana</em>, <em>Cardamine hirsuta</em>, and <em>Draba verna</em>—from the Midi-Pyrenees region of France.
By examining how shared pathogens, physiological trade-offs, and genomic constraints influence resistance gene dynamics, we aim to understand how ancient balanced polymorphisms are maintained. Using R-gene enrichment sequencing, effector mutants, functional assays, and comparative genomics, we explore how molecular interactions between NLR proteins and pathogen effectors drive immune diversity. While some NLRs exhibit high polymorphism in their LRR domains, others show constrained diversity or presence/absence variation, reflecting distinct evolutionary strategies. These responses not only confer pathogen resistance but also shape the composition of plant-associated microbial communities. Ultimately, we aim to develop a comparative framework for understanding immune gene evolution across species and environments.
    </p>
